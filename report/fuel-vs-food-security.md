# Fuel vs food security — the debate, and why the byproduct route dissolves it

*India runs the world's largest food-security programme *and* the world's fastest ethanol ramp — burning
grain for fuel while giving grain away free. Is that a contradiction? The honest answer, and why the
biorefinery thesis sidesteps the trap entirely. July 2026. Companion to
[omss-ethanol-rbo-import-substitution.md](omss-ethanol-rbo-import-substitution.md) and
[executive-summary.md](executive-summary.md).*

## The debate, stated fairly

The tension is real and worth stating at full strength:

- **The food side.** India provides **free grain to ~80 crore people** (NFSA + PMGKAY, ~610 LMT/yr), the
  largest food-security operation on earth. Diverting foodgrain (broken rice, maize, and briefly FCI rice)
  to **ethanol** — now 20% of petrol, 661 crore litres — looks like *burning food for fuel* in a country
  that still has hidden hunger and malnutrition. Critics add: grain-to-ethanol can pull up feed and food
  prices, and rice/sugarcane ethanol is **water-intensive** (a paddy-heavy, water-stressed feedstock).
- **The fuel side.** Ethanol cut the crude-oil import bill (**~₹1.36 lakh cr forex saved**), raised farm
  incomes (₹1.18 lakh cr to farmers), and monetised *surplus and broken* grain that has limited food use.

Both are true. The question is whether they actually collide — and, mostly, they don't.

## Why the collision is smaller than it looks

1. **Ethanol runs mainly on non-PDS, surplus, and non-food feedstock** — sugarcane/molasses, **maize**
   (largely feed/industrial), and **broken rice** (the milling fraction, not issue-grade rice). The grain
   that feeds people is a different stream from the grain that feeds distilleries.
2. **The state already throttles fuel for food.** In **July 2023 the government paused FCI rice sales to
   ethanol distilleries** to protect central-pool stocks and food security — a live demonstration that
   *food outranks fuel when stocks tighten.* The lever is stock-threshold-based in practice; it just isn't
   written as a rule.
3. **Food security is measured in entitlement, not stock-pile size.** The ~80 cr entitlement is protected
   by allocation (610 LMT) held ~2× above buffer norm; ethanol draws on the *surplus above* that, not the
   entitlement. Spoilage is negligible (0.002–0.022% of off-take,
   [utilisation report](warehouse-utilisation-fifo-lifo.md)), so the "grain rots anyway, so burn it" framing
   is also wrong — the grain is fine; the question is its *best use.*

So the sharp version of the debate — "people go hungry so cars can drive" — is not what the data shows.
The real question is narrower: **what is the best use of the surplus and the byproducts?**

## The answer the biorefinery thesis gives: byproducts first — no tradeoff at all

Here is the key move. **The fuel-vs-food debate only exists when you convert the *grain itself*.** It
disappears the moment you convert the **byproduct** instead:

| Route | Feedstock | Competes with food? | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grain → ethanol | broken/surplus **grain** | *partially* — the fuel-vs-food debate lives here | fuel |
| **Bran → rice-bran oil** | the **byproduct** of milling | **No** — the grain still feeds people; only the bran is used | edible oil |
| **Cottonseed → oil** | the **byproduct** of cotton | **No** | edible oil |
| Rice husk → silica/energy | the **byproduct** | **No** | industrial / power |

**Rice-bran oil is the resolution of the debate, not another instance of it.** The paddy is milled and
the rice feeds people through PDS *exactly as before*; the only thing that changes is that the **bran —
today handed to cattle feed with its oil intact** — is processed for oil first. **No grain is diverted from
food. There is no food-vs-fuel tradeoff, because it isn't fuel and it isn't the food grain** — it is the
byproduct substituting an *import* (₹1.68 lakh cr of edible oil), not competing with the ration.

So the project's stance reframes the whole argument: **the choice is not food vs fuel — it is whether to
capture the value of byproducts India already generates and gives away, or keep importing the derivative.**
RBO/edible-oil substitution competes with **imports**, not with the poor's plate.

## A clean decision rule

If policy wants one principle to settle the debate:

1. **Byproducts first** (bran→RBO, cottonseed→oil, husk→silica/energy) — unlimited go-ahead; zero food
   tradeoff, pure import substitution.
2. **Grain-to-fuel only from genuine surplus above a published stock threshold** — institutionalise the
   2023 pause: a transparent rule that diverts grain to ethanol *only* when central-pool stock exceeds
   buffer norm by a stated margin, and pauses automatically when it doesn't.
3. **Never compress the entitlement, never divert issue-grade grain** — the ~80 cr food-security guarantee
   and PDS-grade stock are ring-fenced; only broken/surplus grain and byproducts are in play.
4. **Prefer the least water-intensive feedstock** — maize and byproducts over paddy-based ethanol in
   water-stressed basins.

Under that rule, ethanol and food security coexist, and **the biggest prize — byproduct-based edible-oil
substitution — sits entirely outside the debate.**

## Caveats

The ₹1.36 lakh cr ethanol forex figure is the programme's cumulative crude-substitution claim (PIB/MoPNG),
not an audited net; water-intensity varies sharply by feedstock and basin (sugarcane/paddy high, maize
lower) and is a genuine sustainability caveat this note flags rather than resolves. The 2023 FCI-rice-to-
ethanol pause is public record; whether it is applied consistently is a governance question. RBO's
"no-tradeoff" claim holds because bran is a milling byproduct with the grain's food use unchanged — it does
not extend to *grain*-based ethanol, which this note explicitly keeps inside the debate. Research synthesis
and policy argument, not investment or fiscal advice.
